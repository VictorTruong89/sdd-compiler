1

The idea, and the five painpoints it exists to answer

Spec-driven development lets a human describe a system in ordinary language. That is its whole advantage, and the source of every problem below.

1.1 What this is attachment

Spec-driven development lets a person describe a system in normal but highly ambiguous vocabulary rather than in a strict, structured, skill-gated programming language. That is the point of it. A specification written in English can be read by anyone, argued with by anyone, and changed without a compiler's permission.

The cost is that English has no type checker. Two specifications written three weeks apart can contradict each other completely and nothing anywhere will notice. The larger the spec set becomes, the less likely a human is to notice either — and the more expensive the eventual discovery.

The SDD compiler is a background process that watches a specification set the way make watches a source tree: it maintains a graph of the specification, knows what each change invalidates, and reports what no longer holds.

What it is not

⛔ This is not an attempt to turn SDD into a programming language. There is no grammar to learn, no syntax to satisfy, and no failed build. The borrowed idea from make / cmake is narrower and specific: a dependency graph over units of work, plus a rule for deciding what a change invalidates. The units here are sentences, not object files, and the judge is a language model, not a timestamp.

1.2 The five painpoints attachment

#PainpointWhy it resists a plain LLM prompt
★ P1 Consolidation and conflict resolution across spec sets. Module A has 5 atomic specs, module B has 5. Comparing them honestly is 25 pairwise judgments. Asked in one prompt, the model does not perform 25 comparisons — it performs an impression of them. Hallucination, positional bias and silently skipped pairs all follow. The pair count grows as n²; attention does not.
★ P2 The comparison is semantic, not structural. Two specs conflict by meaning, not by differing on a field. There is no key to join on. String matching cannot see it and embedding similarity actively misleads: a requirement and its negation are near-identical vectors. The judgment has to be an entailment decision made by a model.
P3 As-built code versus the full spec set. The same n² shape, with the ambiguity halved — code is unambiguous, the spec is not. Same explosion, same blocking problem. Half the ambiguity does not halve the pair count.
P4 Carrying a spec set forward across releases while keeping relationships and history intact. Requires versioned identity for every unit of specification, which prose files in git do not provide at the granularity that matters.
P5 Progressive checking during authoring — surface the conflict now, not at the end of the session. A conflict found five minutes after writing is a two-minute fix. The same conflict found across a finished spec set is an archaeology problem.
The through-line

P1 and P2 are the same problem — too many pairs, each requiring a semantic judgment — and they are the only two the first build addresses. P3 is the same machinery pointed at a second corpus. P4 and P5 are product features, not research questions, and §3.4 argues for cutting both out of the first build.

1.3 Why this problem already has a literature

P1 is not novel, which is good news: it is the blocking problem, known for decades in record linkage and entity resolution. When you cannot afford to compare every pair, you generate a smaller candidate set and compare only those — and you measure the tradeoff with three established quantities:

  • Pair completeness — of the true conflict pairs, what fraction survived into the candidate set? (Recall.)
  • Pair quality — of the candidate pairs, what fraction were real conflicts? (Precision.)
  • Reduction ratio — what fraction of the n² pairs never had to be judged at all?

Two further literatures meet it here. Requirements engineering has studied inconsistency detection in natural-language requirements for a long time, mostly with pre-LLM techniques. And the pairwise judgment itself is natural language inference — entailment, contradiction, neutral — which is one of the best-studied tasks in NLP.

⚠ PG-11 applies and is not yet discharged

The three literatures above are named from working knowledge, not from retrieved sources. No DOI in this document has been verified, because none has been fetched. Before any method here is adopted, the PG-11 table in §4.5 must be filled by an actual search — primary source, regime, and one limiting or dissenting paper each. An invented citation in a course submission is academic fraud regardless of what produced it, and the same standard applies here.

The design

A layered graph, an incremental insertion loop with a human in it, and an invalidation rule borrowed from make.

2.1 The insertion loop attachment

The author writes one or two specs in ordinary prose and presses a button. Nothing runs while they type. Two deliberate exclusions define the interaction:

  • ★ No typing-time analysis. Checking on every keystroke is technically harder and, more importantly, interrupts the thought being written down. The button is the commit point.
  • ★ No spec recommendation. The tool does not suggest what to write next. It reports conflicts with what already exists. Suggestion is a different and much harder product, and it would compete with the author's own thinking at exactly the wrong moment.

What the button does:

Why the button design earns its place

Incremental insertion with a human at the commit point closes the atomization-error risk. If the atomizer splits a spec badly, the author sees it immediately, while the source text is still in their head. Batch atomization of a hundred specs hides the same error under a hundred others.

2.2 The layers attachment

Layers exist for human cognition — so the author can see where a statement sits and what it refines — and they do a second job the design depends on: the layer is the blocking key. Conflicts cluster within a layer and along refinement paths; they are rare between an unrelated business goal and an unrelated data model. That is what makes the candidate set small.

Two gaps in the original seven-layer list

1 · Invariants had no home. Rules like no arithmetic inside a prompt or fail loud, never silent are neither business goals nor architecture; they bind everything. Hence the C set — and it is the first thing to build, because it is cheap, needs no labelled data, and produces value on real specs immediately.

2 · tests is a different relation from refines. An L6 node verifies an L1 acceptance criterion. Keep L6 as a layer for the visual if you like, but its edges must be typed separately.

Non-functional requirements — cost ceilings, latency budgets — fold into C.

2.4 Edges are first-class

In the original sketch, relationships lived as node fields (depend_on, conflicts). They should be edges with their own type and their own provenance, because an edge produced by a model judgment needs to record which judgment produced it and when it was last confirmed.

  • refines — crosses layers downward; the backbone of the graph
  • depends_on — within or across layers
  • constrains — from C to any node
  • tests — L6 to the L1 criterion it verifies
  • duplicates / conflicts_with — judged edges, carrying kind, judge_version and a rationale

2.5 Invalidation — the part borrowed from make that was missing

The make analogy was invoked for the graph. The more valuable half is the invalidation rule: when a target changes, which dependents become out of date? Translated here, insertion of node N into a graph of 200 raises two questions:

  1. Which existing nodes must be judged against N? Not all 200 — the blocking claim.
  2. ★ Which previously clean nodes are now stale? When N resolves a conflict, or changes a dependency, earlier verdicts stop being trustworthy.

The second question is what checked_against exists to answer, and it is the one almost nobody implements. It also yields the two measurable claims in §3.2.

2.6 Infrastructure — and why the plugin is built last

⚠ The VS Code extension is a scope hazard

An extension means TypeScript, an extension host, a webview for graph rendering, and diagnostics plumbing — real work that produces no evidence, in a second language, for a repository that is Python.

Decision: engine in Python, with a CLI and a JSON protocol. The extension is a thin client that shells out. Three reasons: the batch evaluation harness (§3.4) then comes nearly free; the engine is importable from this repository as a tools/ module; and if December gets tight the extension can be cut without losing the claim. Roughly 200 lines of TypeScript at the end, not 2,000 at the start.

The button and the batch runner must call the same function. That single constraint is what keeps the demo and the experiment honest about being the same system.

The course project — MIA 5150

The same engine, framed as a claim that can be tested rather than a tool that can be demonstrated.

3.0 Eligibility — is this a foundation-models project? attachment

The brief admits any topic "as long as it works with generative models or foundation models." Nothing in this project trains, fine-tunes or modifies a model, so the question deserves a direct answer rather than an assumption. Three lines in the brief settle it.

  1. "Novel application. Foundation models in a field that has not used them much, with an evaluation built for that field." — Requirements consistency checking is a largely pre-LLM literature. This is close to a textbook instance of the category.
  2. ★ "If your project uses an LLM as part of the method, describe that in the Method section instead." — The decisive line. The brief explicitly anticipates projects where the model is the method rather than a writing aid, and says where to describe it. A rule excluding such projects would not need that sentence to exist.
  3. "Workable: … inference up to about 8B in 4-bit … retrieval over corpora up to about 10 GB." — Inference-only work is listed as workable. No training requirement appears anywhere in the document.

In this project a foundation model performs the atomization and renders every pairwise entailment judgment. All three results are statements about model behaviour, not about a tool:

  • Single-shot whole-corpus comparison silently drops true conflicts — a measured failure mode of long-context generative models, adjacent to the Lost in the Middle finding.
  • Verdicts shift with insertion order — LLM-as-judge reliability: positional bias, self-consistency, verdict stability.
  • ★ A small open model with structured decomposition versus a large model with one prompt — a capability-versus-scaffolding claim, which is squarely a foundation-models result.

The graph is the instrument. The study is about what the instrument measures.

⚠ The real risk is framing, not eligibility

The brief's own contrast: "'We built a chatbot for X' describes something you made; 'we show that retrieval quality matters more than model size for question answering over X' is a claim you can test."

A proposal that opens with a VS Code plugin that builds a graph of your specs reads as a software-engineering tool, and a reader starts hunting for the model. A proposal that opens with foundation-model conflict detection degrades as the specification set grows, and structured decomposition recovers it never raises the question, because the abstract is entirely about model behaviour.

Consequence for naming. The tool keeps its name in this repository; the paper gets a title about models.

  • ✗ An SDD compiler for spec-driven development
  • ✗ A VS Code plugin for specification conflict detection
  • ✓ Structured decomposition for foundation-model consistency checking over natural-language specifications
  • ✓ Does decomposition beat context? Measuring LLM conflict detection as specification sets grow

3.1 The claim attachment

The brief requires a project shaped as a testable claim, not a built artifact. Filled into its four blanks:

The one-sentence test

We claim that graph-structured candidate-pair generation over atomized specifications recovers most true specification conflicts while judging only a small fraction of all pairs, and we will show it by measuring pair completeness, pair quality and reduction ratio, per conflict type against (a) single-shot whole-corpus LLM comparison and (b) exhaustive pairwise LLM judgment on a labelled conflict corpus drawn from a live spec-driven repository and a public requirements corpus.

Category: novel application. Benchmark and empirical study also fits; novel application reads better, because the evaluation is built for a field — requirements engineering — that has not had one. The brief states that a carefully controlled study which finds no improvement still scores well, which is the right frame for a measurement whose outcome is not known in advance.

The claim above is crossed with a second factor — which model renders the judgment — giving three arms × two judges. See §3.2.

3.2 The arms, and the second claim incrementality unlocks attachment

ArmWhat it isRole
(a) Single-shotPaste both spec sets into one prompt and ask what conflicts — current practiceThe honest baseline. This is where hallucination, positional bias and skipped pairs actually come from.
(b) Exhaustive pairwiseAll n(n−1)/2 pairs judged independentlyThe recall ceiling. Expensive, but affordable at this n.
★ (c) Graph-blockedAtomize → layer → edge → candidate pairs → judge only thoseThe contribution. Claim: approaches (b)'s recall while judging a small fraction of the pairs.

★ The crossed factor: which model renders the judgment

Each arm runs under two judges — a large hosted model and a 7–8B open-weights model in 4-bit, run locally. Three arms × two judges is a proper factorial and it buys three things that a hosted-only design cannot:

  1. The compute budget becomes a real budget. A hosted-only project consumes roughly zero GPU-hours, which sits oddly in a course whose hardware guidance is entirely about GPU memory. The local arm produces genuine GPU-hour arithmetic, comfortably inside the ~20-hour ceiling and squarely inside the brief's "workable" list.
  2. Reproducibility stops depending on anyone's API credits. The brief requires a code link whose README reproduces the main table. A reader with a consumer GPU can run the open-weights half end to end.
  3. ★ It adds the most interesting result in the study. Does graph-structured decomposition let a small open model match a large hosted one at conflict detection? That finding is worth reporting in either direction, and it is unambiguously a foundation-models finding rather than a tooling one.

Cost: one extra column in the main table. It closes the eligibility question in §3.0 permanently.

⚠ Do not lead with cost savings

At 137 acceptance criteria the exhaustive arm is 9,316 pairs — on a small fast model at roughly 500 tokens per judgment, that is a low-double-digit dollar figure for a full run. A headline of "$15 reduced to $2" persuades nobody. The result that lands is (a) versus (b)/(c): that one-shot comparison over a whole corpus silently drops most true conflicts. That is the painpoint, stated as a number, and it is rarely measured.

★ The second claim: incremental insertion is the contribution, not the UX

Because insertion is incremental and every press is traced, two further properties become measurable — and neither is available to a batch-only system:

  • Invalidation efficiency — does incremental checking with dependency-based invalidation reach the same conflict recall as a full re-check, at a fraction of the judgments?
  • ★ Order independence (confluence) — does the final conflict set depend on the order the specs were entered? Replay the same node set under ~20 shuffled insertion orders and measure set agreement. If it is order-dependent, that is a real and honest finding about incremental model-judged graphs.
Why this forces the batch harness to exist

Order independence can only be measured offline, by replay. The live button can never produce that number. This is the technical reason the demo and the experiment must be two entry points into one engine, and it is worth stating in the proposal.

3.4 ★★ Demo and experiment are different artifacts

The single largest risk to the mark

A live demonstration over 10–20 atomic specs is the right thing to show — legible, kinetic, lands the idea in ninety seconds. It is not evidence. The brief marks quality of evidence at 30% of the presentation and evaluation rigour at 25% of the report, and at n = 15 there are roughly 105 pairs and perhaps three to six true conflicts: one missed conflict swings recall by twenty points. There is no statistical power at that size.

HarnessWhat runsProduces
DemoPlugin, 10–20 specs, live, conflicts surfacing on the buttonTwo minutes of the talk; one figure in the report
★ BatchSame engine, headless, over the full labelled corpus; replays insertion ordersThe main table. All three arms, all metrics, all seeds

One engine, two entry points. The batch harness is roughly a day of work if the engine has a clean API from the start, and it is the difference between a demonstration and a study.

3.5 Ground truth — three sources

Someone must decide that two specs conflict. Incremental checking does not answer this; it changes when you look, not whether you know the right answer. Three sources together:

  1. ★ Adjudication log. Every button press records source text, atomizer version, nodes created, pairs judged, verdicts raised, and the author's ruling on each alert. Nine weeks of ordinary spec writing deposits a human-labelled corpus as a by-product. This is the primary source and it costs nothing beyond logging.
  2. Injected conflicts. Take a clean spec set, mutate criteria into known contradictions, measure detection. Gives controlled recall. ⚠ Design the mutations so they are not trivially recognisable as mutations.
  3. A public requirements corpus. External validity, so the main table is not single-source.

⚠ Two constraints to settle before the proposal

Compute — two currencies, and the proposal must show both. The hosted judge is billed in tokens: per-judgment token cost × pair count × arms × repeats. The open-weights judge is billed in GPU-hours: pair count × arms × repeats × seconds per judgment ÷ 3600, on a 7–8B model in 4-bit inside the ~15 GB envelope. The second figure is what the brief's compute section is actually asking for, and the exhaustive arm is what makes it non-trivial — 137 criteria is 9,316 pairs, so the replay factor for the order-independence experiment must be chosen with the ceiling in mind, not discovered against it. ⚠ Every paid run needs explicit approval before it executes.

Code disclosure. The brief requires a public code link with a README and seeds. That is in direct tension with keeping the product private. Resolve it by splitting: publish the evaluation harness and the measured blocking component — enough to reproduce the main table — and keep the shipped layer schema, the editor integration and the product surface out of it. Decide this before the proposal, because it changes what is promised in it.

3.8 Working alone not for the attachment

Two reasons hold up, and one does not.

  • ★ Commercial intent. Intended for commercial use after the course; sole authorship keeps ownership unambiguous. Joint authorship on a three-person course project creates real ambiguity under university IP policy. This is the strongest available reason and the only one that cannot be argued with.
  • Unequal prior context. The project builds on an existing personal repository — 11 specifications, 137 acceptance criteria, a governance process — which supplies the labelled corpus but would put teammates at an unequal starting point and make the contributions appendix lopsided.
  • ⛔ Do not write: "meetings waste time and I cannot control others' progress." It reads as impatience with collaborators and invites the reply that coordination is part of the exercise. The same underlying fact is stated correctly as the second bullet.
4

Governance, decisions, and what is still open

Where the tool lives in this repository, what has been settled, and what has not.

4.1 Where this lives — and why not in src/

The same three reasons that keep the literature loop out of src/ apply here without modification:

  • PG-8 — this repository's tree must remain a superset of the pipeline's src/** so absorption is a merge and not a restructuring. A spec tool under src/ pollutes that merge for zero benefit.
  • No seat, no domain. It produces nothing anyone would trade on, so it sits outside the authority model entirely — which is precisely why it needs no governance.
  • Vision §7 lists durable non-goals about the product. A dev-time tool does not violate them, but placing it in src/ would make it look as though it did, to a future reader who has only the tree.

So: tools/sdd_compiler/, or a skill wrapping it — the same category as fmp-api: hand-built, dev-time, authoritative about something external, governing nothing. Its outputs land in sdd/design/.

⚠ The drift to guard against

This tool has commercial intent and will grow. Do not let it acquire tendrils into governed paths. If any part of it is ever consumed by something on the path to a number someone trades on, it enters under a spec with acceptance criteria, a trace and evaluation — not as a tools/ import.

4.3 Rejected alternatives

Rejections are worth as much as decisions — they stop the next session re-proposing them.

  • Rejected — typing-time analysis. ✗ Technically harder and it interrupts the thought being written. The button is the commit point.
  • Rejected — spec recommendation / autocomplete. ✗ A different and much harder product, competing with the author's own thinking at the worst moment.
  • Rejected — embedding similarity as the blocking key. ✗ A requirement and its negation embed near-identically. Layer and edge structure are the blocking signal; similarity may supplement but must never be the only one.
  • Rejected — turning SDD into a formal specification language. ✗ Destroys the one advantage SDD has. The ambiguity is the feature; the tool exists to survive it, not to remove it.
  • Rejected — the live demo as the evaluation. ✗ §3.4. It is a demonstration, which the brief explicitly names as the wrong shape of project.
  • Rejected — leading the course claim with cost reduction. ✗ The absolute dollar figures are too small to impress. The accuracy gap between single-shot and pairwise is the finding.
  • Rejected — hand-authored family groupings. ✗ Rots. Derive and cache.

4.4 Open questions

  • ★ Which public requirements corpus? Blocks the n-too-small risk and the proposal's evaluation plan. Needs an answer by late September.
  • Which specific open-weights model? Settled in principle as a crossed factor (§3.2); the remaining question is which 7–8B model in 4-bit fits the ~15 GB envelope with a long enough context for two atomic specs plus instructions, and whether it is competent at subsumption rather than only at flat contradiction.
  • How many replays for the order-independence experiment? Directly multiplies the GPU-hour budget. Needs a power argument, not a round number.
